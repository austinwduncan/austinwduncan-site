Romans 3:21–22 and the courtroom language at the center of the gospel
Some church words sound familiar long before they are understood
There are some words in the Bible that most Christians feel like they understand simply because they have heard them for years.

They know where the word usually appears. They know the kind of sermon it belongs to. They know the basic idea it seems to carry. So the word becomes familiar before it becomes clear.

“Righteousness” is one of those words.

If you ask most Christians what righteousness means, the answers usually sound something like this: being good, doing what is right, moral purity, holiness, obedience, or living the way God wants you to live. And those answers are not completely wrong. Scripture does speak about righteousness in moral terms in some places. Righteousness is not less than what is right.

But in Paul, especially in Romans 3 and 4, the word is doing more than many Christians first realize. It is not mainly describing a quality you slowly build up in yourself. It is not first functioning as a report card. It is functioning as a legal standing. A declared status. A verdict.

And if that sounds too technical at first, it won’t once you start paying attention to the passage.

Because Paul is not talking like a teacher grading students.

He is talking like a judge rendering a verdict.

That is one of the most important things to understand if you want to understand the gospel the way Paul explains it.

The gospel gets distorted when righteousness sounds like a score
This matters because if righteousness mainly means moral achievement in your mind, then your whole experience of the Christian life begins to tilt in a certain direction.

You start thinking of your standing before God as something that rises and falls with your performance. A good week gives you confidence. A bad week makes you feel distant. Strong prayer, consistent Bible reading, visible obedience, and emotional steadiness make you feel like you are doing better. Failure, sin, distraction, coldness, and weakness make you feel like you are doing worse.

That is how grades work.

And a surprising number of Christians live before God as though he is always grading them.

Not always in doctrine. Often they know the right theology on paper. But in lived experience, they still act as though their standing with God is being recalculated every few days.

That is not how Paul talks.

And the reason it matters is not merely theoretical. If you are living under a grade, you will never be at peace for long. You may have brief moments of confidence, but they will always be fragile. They will always depend on how the last few days went. You will always be one failure away from feeling spiritually unsafe again.

But if Paul is describing righteousness as a verdict, then the entire atmosphere changes.

Not because sin becomes unimportant.

Not because holiness stops mattering.

But because the ground beneath the believer is no longer their performance.

It is the declaration of God in Christ.

Romans 3 introduces righteousness as something manifested and received
Paul’s wording already tells you he means more than moral quality
Romans 3:21–22 says, “But now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law… the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe.”

That sentence is worth slowing down for.

Notice the language Paul uses.

This righteousness is manifested.

It is revealed.

It comes through faith.

It is for all who believe.

That is already a clue.

Because if “the righteousness of God” means only God’s own moral perfection, then the sentence becomes hard to hear properly. God’s moral perfection is not something believers receive in the same way a gift is received. And if the phrase means only the moral standard God requires, then the sentence still feels off. Standards are not given to people through faith as a gracious status. Standards confront. Standards expose. Standards condemn when they are unmet.

But Paul is describing something that has now appeared apart from the law, though the Law and the Prophets bear witness to it. He is talking about something God has brought forward. Something announced. Something granted. Something received.

That should make us stop.

Because it means δικαιοσύνη here is not operating primarily as a description of God’s character or merely as the standard he requires. It is functioning as the right standing God gives.

That does not mean God’s own righteousness disappears from view. It means Paul is focusing here on the righteousness that comes from God to the believer.

And once you see that, the whole paragraph starts tightening.

Paul is not announcing a better path to moral success
It is important to remember where Paul is in the flow of Romans.

He has already spent a long time proving that human beings do not establish their own right standing before God by law, morality, or religious identity. Jew and Gentile alike are under sin. Every mouth is stopped. The law cannot rescue. It can expose, but it cannot justify.

So when Paul says, “But now,” he is announcing a decisive turn.

Not, “But now people have finally found a way to become good enough.”

Not, “But now the law has been explained more clearly so you can achieve what was required.”

No. Paul is saying God has acted.

A righteousness from God has now been manifested apart from the law.

That means the whole paragraph is leaning away from self-built moral standing and toward a status granted by God in Christ.

That is why courtroom language fits so well.

Because Paul is not describing a student slowly improving his grade. He is describing a guilty person standing in court and hearing a verdict he could never have produced for himself.

The family of Greek words behind righteous, righteousness, justify, and justification
One word becomes clearer when you see the whole group
One of the best ways to understand what Paul means by righteousness is to stop looking only at δικαιοσύνη in isolation and instead look at the family of Greek words behind righteous, righteousness, justify, and justification.

Once you put them together, the setting becomes much easier to see.

There is δίκαιος, meaning righteous or just. That is the adjective. It describes the one whose standing is right.

There is δικαιόω, meaning to justify or declare righteous. That is the verb. It is the courtroom action. The judge renders a favorable verdict.

There is δικαιοσύνη, the noun, meaning righteousness or right standing. That is the status of the one who has been declared right.

There is δικαίωμα, meaning a righteous requirement, a just decree, or a legal claim. That is the legitimate demand of the law.

And there is δικαίωσις, meaning justification. That is the act of being declared righteous, the judicial event itself.

Now once you set those next to each other, the pattern is hard to miss.

This is courtroom language.

Judge language.

Verdict language.

Status language.

Requirement language.

Paul is not pulling vocabulary from the world of classroom evaluation or inner moral self-improvement. He is pulling vocabulary from the world of law, judgment, declaration, and standing.

That does not erase moral transformation from the Christian life. But it does tell you that in Romans 3 and 4, Paul is dealing first with the question of legal standing before God.

The whole word group moves in one direction
This is where the whole passage begins to clarify.

There is a righteous requirement.

There is a judge who justifies.

There is a justification that happens.

There is a resulting righteousness, meaning right standing.

There is a person who is declared righteous.

All of that belongs together.

Once you see that, it becomes much harder to reduce righteousness to “being a morally better person.”

That is too small.

Righteousness in Paul is not first an achievement you display to the court. It is the standing you receive from the court when the judge declares you right.

And that makes Romans 3 sound different immediately.

A courtroom is not a classroom
This may be the simplest way to say the whole point
A classroom gives grades.

A courtroom gives verdicts.

A grade tells you how well you performed.

A verdict tells you your standing before the law.

A grade rises and falls with the quality of your work.

A verdict, once rendered, stands unless overturned.

That difference is not small. It changes the whole emotional tone of a person’s life.

If you think your standing before God works like a grade, then you will live in constant evaluation. Every day becomes a test. Every week becomes a report card. Every failure becomes a drop in standing. Every success becomes a temporary rise.

That is exhausting.

But if righteousness is a verdict, then the question changes. The issue is no longer, “How did I do this week?” The issue becomes, “What has God declared in Christ?”

That does not remove the need for repentance.

It does not make obedience optional.

But it does relocate the ground under your feet.

And that is what Paul is doing.

Why so many Christians miss this
Part of the reason Christians miss this is because morality is easier to picture than verdict. Most of us know what it feels like to succeed or fail. We know what it feels like to compare, to improve, to fall short, to try harder. So it is very natural to drag all of that into our spiritual life and assume God must be responding to us in the same way.

But Paul is saying something stranger and better than that.

He is saying that in Christ, God the Judge renders a verdict over the ungodly person who believes.

That person is not nearly righteous.

Not potentially righteous.

Not probationarily righteous.

Righteous.

That is courtroom language. And it is one of the most offensive and freeing things the gospel says.

Martin Luther helps us feel what is at stake
Luther heard “the righteousness of God” as a threat
This is where the story of Martin Luther is so helpful.

Before Luther came to understand Romans, he hated the phrase “the righteousness of God.” Not because he took God lightly, but because he took God seriously. He was a monk, disciplined, intense, and deeply troubled. He confessed constantly, fasted, prayed, examined himself, and still found no peace.

Why?

Because he heard “the righteousness of God” as the perfect standard of a holy God that condemned him.

So when Romans 1:17 said the righteousness of God is revealed in the gospel, Luther did not hear good news. He heard the standard made even more visible, which only made him despair more. If the gospel was just the unveiling of what God requires, then the gospel itself became terrifying.

That is what happens when righteousness sounds only like a grade.

You hear the measure, and you already know you have failed it.

Then he saw that righteousness was gift
What changed everything for Luther was not that he became less serious about holiness. It was that he realized Paul was talking not only about the righteousness God is, but about the righteousness God gives.

Not just the standard.

The status.

Not just what God demands.

What God grants.

And when that clicked, Luther said it felt like he had gone through open gates into paradise. Scripture itself opened up to him because he realized the gospel was not merely revealing the standard that condemns. It was also announcing the verdict that justifies.

That is the difference between living under law as grade and hearing righteousness as gift.

And that is why this matters so much. It is not a minor word-study detail. It changes the emotional and spiritual world of the believer.

Romans 4 makes the logic unmistakable
Abraham becomes Paul’s great example
If Romans 3 introduces the argument, Romans 4 presses it home so hard that it becomes almost impossible to misunderstand.

Paul goes to Abraham and says, “Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness.”

That word “counted” matters a great deal. It is an accounting word. A reckoning word. A bookkeeping word. Something is being credited to Abraham’s account.

That already tells you righteousness here is not mainly a moral quality Abraham produced on his own. It is something reckoned to him. Counted to him.

Then Paul sharpens the contrast even further.

“To the one who works, his wages are not counted as a gift but as his due.”

That is obvious enough. If you earn wages, you do not call them grace. They are payment.

Then Paul says, “And to the one who does not work but believes in him who justifies the ungodly, his faith is counted as righteousness.”

That sentence is explosive.

God justifies the ungodly
Not the respectable.

Not the almost-there.

Not the morally promising.

The ungodly.

That matters because it settles something essential. God’s verdict of righteousness is not the reward for someone who has made themselves worthy. It is the declaration spoken over the ungodly person who believes.

That is why righteousness here cannot mean “the grade you finally earned.”

The person did not earn it.

Paul is explicit. The one who does not work but believes.

That means the verdict is not the recognition of human achievement.

It is the gracious declaration of God.

And the moment you see that, the whole logic of justification becomes cleaner.

Faith is not the last little work that earns the verdict
This is a place people often get tangled.

They hear that righteousness is received by faith, and then they quietly turn faith into the one final acceptable work. The one thing they did correctly enough that God rewarded them.

Paul will not allow that reading.

Faith is not wages.

Faith is not merit.

Faith is not the one impressive contribution the sinner makes.

The whole point of Romans 4 is that the ungodly person brings nothing but need and receives everything as gift.

That is why righteousness is counted.

That is why it is grace.

That is why boasting is excluded.

The believer is not justified because faith is so worthy. The believer is justified because God counts righteousness to the one who believes.

Philippians 3 names the two options clearly
Paul speaks of two righteousnesses
Philippians 3 is one of the clearest passages in the New Testament for this whole issue.

Paul lays out his religious credentials. He has ancestry, seriousness, law observance, zeal, and public respectability. If anyone could try to stand before God on the basis of résumé, it was Paul.

And then he says he counts it all as loss. Refuse. Worthless as a ground of confidence.

Why?

Because he wants to “be found in him, not having a righteousness of my own that comes from the law, but that which comes through faith… the righteousness from God.”

There are the two options.

A righteousness of my own.

And a righteousness from God.

That is one of the most important contrasts in all of Paul.

One righteousness is built, the other is received
A righteousness of my own is what many people, religious or not, are always trying to construct. It says, “Look at what I’ve done. Look at my obedience. Look at my seriousness. Look at my consistency. Grade me on that.”

That kind of righteousness is always unstable. It has good days and bad days. It is anxious by nature because it is never finished. It always needs one more week of obedience, one more stretch of consistency, one more proof that you are doing enough.

But Paul says he wants something else.

The righteousness from God.

That phrase matters so much.

From God.

Not self-generated.

Not self-improved.

Not eventually recognized because it matured enough.

From God.

Which means the whole basis of standing has moved outside the self. That is exactly what the gospel does. It relocates the ground.

Romans 3:26 is where the last two weeks come together
“Just and the justifier” belongs with the mercy seat
This is where Week 7 and Week 8 really lock together.

Romans 3:25 says God put Christ forward as ἱλαστήριον, the mercy seat, the place where atonement is made.

Then verse 26 explains why.

“So that he might be just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus.”

That sentence is the hinge.

God is just.

And God justifies.

Both words come from the same family of Greek words behind righteous, righteousness, justify, and justification.

That means Paul is not changing the subject. He is showing you the point of the cross.

How can God remain just and still declare the ungodly righteous?

That is the question.

And the answer is not that God simply decides to look the other way. It is not that he lowers the standard. It is not that he chooses mercy at the expense of justice.

The answer is the cross.

The cross is where justice and justification meet
A just judge cannot ignore sin.

A just judge cannot pretend the case is not real.

A just judge cannot declare a person righteous by denying what is true.

And yet Paul says God justifies the ungodly.

How can both be true?

The ἱλαστήριον.

The mercy seat.

The place where blood is applied.

The place where the just claims of God are fully honored and the favorable verdict is spoken over the sinner who believes.

That is why verse 26 matters so much. It shows that justification is not God becoming less just. It is God remaining just while justifying through Christ.

And once you see that, last week’s mercy-seat language and this week’s courtroom language snap together.

The mercy seat is the place where the verdict is made possible.

The cross is the place where justice is upheld and the believer is declared righteous.

That is Paul’s argument.

This changes how Christians experience their standing before God
Many believers still live as though they are being graded
This is where doctrine becomes pastoral care.

A lot of Christians know the language of grace, faith, and justification, but in daily life they still think in grades.

You can tell by what happens after a bad week.

They feel spiritually unsafe.

God feels farther away.

Prayer feels harder because they assume they have lost ground.

Their confidence shrinks because they are reading the week as a lower score.

That is grade language.

And if that is the atmosphere you live in, then peace will always be temporary. A strong week may make you feel safer for a little while, but the next failure will undo it.

That is no way to live.

And Paul is not leaving Christians there.

The verdict does not rise and fall with your performance
A grade fluctuates.

That is what grades do.

A verdict, once rendered, stands.

That is why Romans 8:1 matters so much in this discussion.

“There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus.”

That is verdict language again.

No condemnation.

Not reduced condemnation.

Not delayed condemnation.

Not condemnation under review.

No condemnation.

That means the believer’s standing is not being recalculated every morning based on yesterday’s performance. The case has been decided in Christ.

That does not make sin less serious. It does not make repentance less necessary. It does mean your failures do not reopen the courtroom and force the judge to rethink the verdict every week.

That is a very different way to live.

This does not make holiness less important
Verdict comes before transformation, not instead of it
It is important to say what Paul is not saying.

He is not saying holiness does not matter.

He is not saying moral transformation is optional.

He is not saying God does not care how his people live.

He is saying that justification and sanctification are not the same thing.

Justification is God’s verdict of right standing.

Sanctification is the ongoing work of moral and spiritual transformation.

If you confuse them, you will either become self-righteous or anxious or both. But if you keep them in the right order, the Christian life begins to make more sense.

God declares.

Then God transforms.

He does not wait for transformation to become good enough before he declares.

Christians obey from acceptance, not for acceptance
That may be the cleanest way to put it.

If righteousness is a grade, then obedience becomes a strategy for acceptance.

If righteousness is a verdict, then obedience becomes the life of a person who has already been accepted in Christ.

Those are two completely different spiritual environments.

One produces anxiety, performance, and instability.

The other produces gratitude, repentance, freedom, and actual growth.

That is why this is not some abstract theological distinction. It changes the entire atmosphere of discipleship.

Tracing the whole group of related Greek words helps the meaning settle
One word becomes clearer when the surrounding words are in view
This is why the study skill for this week matters so much.

When you find a theologically important word in Paul, do not stop with that one word. Trace the whole group of related Greek words around it.

In this case, you look at the words behind righteous, righteousness, justify, justification, and righteous requirement.

Once you do that, the full picture becomes much clearer.

There is the law’s rightful claim.

There is the act of justifying.

There is the status of righteousness.

There is the person declared righteous.

There is God as the just one who does the declaring.

That is not random vocabulary. That is a carefully structured legal world.

Paul builds arguments with related words
Paul is a careful writer. When he clusters related words together, he is not doing that by accident. He is making you hear the connections.

Romans 3:26 is a perfect example.

God is just.

And God justifies.

Same word group.

Same courtroom.

Same gospel.

That is why tracing related words is such a useful habit. It keeps you from flattening a big idea into one thin English definition. It lets the whole field of meaning come into view.

Final thoughts
There really are two ways to stand before God
In the end, Paul gives you two ways of thinking about your standing before God.

One is a righteousness of your own.

A self-built righteousness.

A righteousness of effort, progress, résumé, consistency, law-keeping, and performance.

That righteousness is anxious by nature. It has to be. It rises and falls with how things are going. It never feels finished because it is never finished.

The other is the righteousness from God.

The righteousness counted to the believer.

The righteousness declared in Christ.

The righteousness that comes as verdict, not as grade.

That righteousness does not wobble every time your week does. It is grounded in the cross, where God is both just and the justifier.

You are not graded. You are declared
And that may be the line worth carrying with you from all of this.

You are not graded.

You are declared.

That does not make holiness optional.

It makes holiness possible without fear being the center of it.

Because once the verdict is in, the Christian life is no longer a desperate effort to become acceptable enough for God. It becomes the life of someone who already stands accepted in Christ and now learns to walk from that place.

That is why Luther felt like paradise had opened when he understood this.

Not because his behavior had suddenly become perfect.

But because he finally understood what ground he was standing on.

And that is what Paul is saying in Romans 3 and 4.

Righteousness is not your score.

It is God’s verdict in Christ.
