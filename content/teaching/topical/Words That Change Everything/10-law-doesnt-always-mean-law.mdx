Romans 3:28, James 2:24, and why the same word can sound like a contradiction when it isn’t
Some of the hardest Bible problems are really reading problems
There are places in the Bible where the tension feels immediate. You do not need a commentary to notice it. You do not need Greek to see that something sounds off. Two verses sit next to each other in your mind, and they appear to say opposite things.

Romans 3:28 says, “For we hold that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law.”

James 2:24 says, “You see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone.”

Put those together without context, and it sounds like a flat contradiction.

Faith apart from works.

Works, not faith alone.

Same subject. Same general theological territory. Apparently opposite conclusions.

That is exactly why this passage matters so much for the whole semester. Because it forces us to use almost every skill we have been building. You cannot solve this by grabbing one verse harder. You cannot solve it by pretending the tension is not real. And you cannot solve it by flattening two very different authors into one voice.

You solve it by reading carefully.

That is the whole point of this week.

And one of the keys is a single Greek word: νόμος.

But before we get there, it helps to say plainly what kind of problem this is.

This is not a problem with the Bible.

It is a problem that shows up when readers assume that the same word must mean the same thing in every author, every setting, every argument, and every sentence.

That assumption will get you in trouble fast.

And it is exactly what we are going to avoid tonight.

Luther helps us see how easy it is to get this wrong
Even very great readers can impose one writer’s categories onto another
It is worth starting with Martin Luther, because Luther was one of the most important readers of Paul in church history. He understood something in Romans that changed his life and, in many ways, changed the course of Western Christianity. When he saw that righteousness in Paul is received rather than achieved, the gates opened for him. That was real insight. That mattered.

But Luther also had a hard time with James.

He famously called James an epistle of straw. He did not think highly of it. He felt that James did not preach Christ clearly enough, and he heard James’s language about works as a contradiction to Paul’s gospel of justification by faith.

Luther was wrong about James.

And his mistake is instructive.

He read James through Pauline categories without first asking whether James was addressing the same problem Paul was addressing.

That is the key.

Luther had learned from Paul that the sinner is justified by faith apart from works of the law. That was true. But then when James said a person is justified by works and not by faith alone, Luther heard James as though James must be denying Paul’s point.

He was not.

James was using similar words in a different argument for a different audience against a different danger.

That is why this matters so much. If Luther could make that mistake, so can anyone. And churches do, all the time.

The problem is not that James and Paul disagree about the gospel
The problem is that readers often fail to ask the right questions before deciding what a word means.

Who wrote this?

To whom?

What problem are they solving?

How does this author use this word elsewhere?

What sense of the word fits this passage?

If you skip those questions and rush straight to a theological conclusion, you can easily manufacture contradictions the text itself is not making.

So this week is not just about Paul and James.

It is about how to read the Bible when two verses feel like they are pulling in opposite directions.

Start with the apparent contradiction and let it feel sharp
The tension is real at the surface level
It is worth letting the problem sit there for a minute without smoothing it too quickly.

Romans 3:28 says one is justified by faith apart from works of the law.

James 2:24 says a person is justified by works and not by faith alone.

That is not a minor difference in wording.

Paul says apart from works.

James says not by faith alone.

Paul seems to be pushing works out of the equation.

James seems to be pushing works into the equation.

If those were written by the same author in the same letter to the same group of people, you would have a real problem.

But they are not.

That matters.

Because meaning is not determined by isolated words alone. Meaning is determined by words in context, used by particular authors, addressing particular people, in response to particular issues.

That is where the tension starts to soften.

Not because we are trying to dodge the hard part, but because the hard part actually lies in context, not outside it.

Shared vocabulary does not guarantee shared meaning
This is one of the biggest lessons in all of word study.

The same Greek word can do different things in different mouths.

That does not mean language becomes unstable. It means language is living and contextual.

Take νόμος.

Sometimes it refers to the Mosaic Torah.

Sometimes it refers to a principle or norm.

Sometimes it refers to a summary ethical demand.

Sometimes it refers to a governing power or operative force.

And that variation can happen not only across different authors, but even within the same letter.

If that is true, then you cannot solve Paul and James by assuming that “law” means the same thing in both places. And once you stop assuming that, the path through the passage begins to open.

Paul’s problem in Romans is not James’s problem
Romans is about covenant membership, justification, and boasting
Paul’s concern in Romans 3 is very specific.

He is writing into a mixed community of Jewish and Gentile believers. One of the great questions running underneath Romans is this: on what basis are people included among the people of God? How do Gentiles stand in the covenant? Are they fully included through Christ, or must they take on Jewish covenant markers in order to belong?

That is not a minor historical detail. It is the atmosphere of the argument.

In Paul’s world, Torah was not simply a collection of abstract moral principles floating in midair. It was also the covenant charter of Israel. It marked out who the people of God were. Circumcision, dietary laws, sabbath, purity practices, and the whole shape of Jewish life functioned as identity markers. They were not merely personal devotions. They marked the boundaries of the covenant community.

So when Paul argues that one is justified by faith apart from works of the law, he is not mainly arguing against generic moral effort in the abstract. He is arguing that covenant status before God does not come through Torah observance as boundary-marking identity performance. Gentiles do not become Jews first in order to belong to Christ.

That is the context.

“Works of the law” is not a vague phrase in Paul
This is part of why the phrase “works of the law” matters so much.

Paul does not merely say “works.” He says “works of the law.”

That phrase has a specific edge to it. It points to works done in relation to the law, especially in the context of covenant identity, boasting, and exclusion.

Paul’s argument is not that moral transformation does not matter. It clearly does in Romans 6, 8, 12, and beyond. His argument is that law-observant identity markers do not justify and do not secure covenant inclusion.

That is why boasting is excluded in Romans 3. If people could stand before God on the basis of Torah-defined performance, then boasting would remain. But Paul says boasting is shut out, because justification is by faith.

So the fight Paul is having is not against obedience as such.

It is against the use of law observance as the basis of right standing and covenant inclusion.

That is a very specific problem.

And it is not the problem James is addressing.

Paul himself shows that νόμος shifts within Romans
He does not use the word in only one sense
This is one of the clearest reasons you cannot flatten νόμος into a single meaning.

Even inside Romans, Paul uses the same word in multiple ways.

In Romans 3:21, “apart from the law,” νόμος is clearly the Mosaic law.

In Romans 3:27, Paul says, “By what kind of law? By a law of works? No, but by the law of faith.” There νόμος is functioning more like principle or rule, not Torah in a narrow sense.

In Romans 7:22, Paul says, “I delight in the law of God.” There νόμος is again the divine law, something holy and good.

In Romans 7:21 to 23, Paul can speak of another “law” at work in his members, or the law of his mind, using the term for operative power or governing principle.

In Romans 8:2, “the law of the Spirit of life” and “the law of sin and death” both use νόμος, but neither refers simply to the Mosaic code. Paul is using the word for ruling power, operative regime, or controlling principle.

Then in Romans 13:8, love fulfills the law.

So even within Paul, νόμος is not one flat category.

That matters because if Paul himself uses the same word flexibly depending on context, we should not be surprised when James uses the same word differently again.

Context is not optional with a word like νόμος
This is exactly the kind of word that punishes careless reading.

If you decide that νόμος always means Mosaic Torah in the same sense every time, Romans will become a mess.

If you decide that it always means moral principle, Romans will still become a mess.

The only way forward is context.

What is Paul talking about here?

What contrast is he making?

What problem is he addressing?

What does the surrounding argument demand?

Those questions are not extras. They are the only way to read responsibly.

And once you get used to asking them, the text starts making a lot more sense.

James is fighting a different battle entirely
James is not arguing about Gentile inclusion
Now turn to James.

James opens by addressing “the twelve tribes in the Dispersion.” That tells you immediately that his audience and atmosphere are different. He is writing to Jewish Christians. He is not dealing with the same Jew-Gentile covenant-membership question that dominates Romans and Galatians.

That matters more than many readers realize.

James is not trying to answer whether Gentiles need circumcision.

He is not trying to settle whether boundary markers define the people of God.

He is not fighting Judaizers.

He is fighting dead faith.

That is his problem.

And if you miss that, you will misread the whole letter.

James is addressing people who say the right things and do nothing
Read James on his own terms and the pastoral concern becomes obvious.

People hear the word and do not do it.

People show favoritism in the assembly.

People bless God with their mouth and curse people made in God’s image.

People see brothers or sisters hungry and cold and answer with pious words instead of practical help.

People claim faith, but the claimed faith produces nothing living.

That is why James says even demons believe and shudder.

His concern is not whether a person can be justified by Torah observance.

His concern is whether what someone is calling “faith” is actually alive at all.

That is a very different problem.

And because it is a different problem, the language of law and works lands differently in his letter.

James’s use of νόμος sounds different because his pastoral aim is different
“The perfect law, the law of liberty”
This is where the difference becomes really clear.

James 1:25 speaks of “the perfect law, the law of liberty.”

That is an astonishing phrase if you have only Paul’s polemical use of law in your ears. James can call the law perfect and liberating, not because he is teaching salvation by Torah observance, but because he is speaking of the law as the shape of obedient life under God, fulfilled in love and enacted in doing.

He is not talking about law as covenant boundary badge.

He is talking about law as the pattern of free obedience.

That is why “law of liberty” does not sound like Romans 3. James is standing in a different place, addressing a different danger.

“The royal law” is love of neighbor
Then James 2:8 calls it “the royal law,” and he quotes, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”

That is crucial.

James is not, in that moment, talking about circumcision, food laws, or sabbath identity markers. He is talking about love of neighbor as the summary of the law’s demand.

And that means when James criticizes a faith that does not work, he is not saying, “Faith is insufficient unless you add Torah badges.” He is saying, “If your faith does not produce love, mercy, and embodied obedience, then what you are calling faith is dead.”

That is a very different claim.

And once you see it, James stops sounding like a rival to Paul and starts sounding like a pastor guarding a different flank of the same gospel.

What James means by “works”
James is not talking about works of Torah boundary-marking
This is where a lot of confusion lifts.

When James says a person is justified by works and not by faith alone, the works he has in view are not the same thing Paul means by “works of the law” in Romans 3.

James’s works are the visible actions of obedient, living faith.

Feeding the hungry.

Caring for the vulnerable.

Showing no favoritism.

Acting on what one claims to believe.

Abraham offering Isaac.

Rahab hiding the spies.

These are not covenant-entry badges.

They are not ethnic identity practices.

They are not the ritual markers Paul is excluding as the basis of justification.

They are the lived fruit of a faith that is real.

That distinction matters a lot.

James is attacking verbal faith without embodied obedience
James’s burden is that there is a kind of faith that is only verbal. A faith that can say correct things and remain unmoved by need. A faith that is orthodox in statement and dead in action.

That is why James says, “Show me your faith apart from your works, and I will show you my faith by my works.”

He is not saying works replace faith.

He is saying works reveal whether the faith being claimed is alive.

That is why James can speak the way he does. He is not answering Paul’s question. He is answering a different question.

Paul asks, “On what basis is a sinner justified before God?”

James asks, “What does real faith look like in the life of the person who claims to believe?”

Once those questions are distinguished, the contradiction begins to disappear.

Paul and James are using “justify” differently too
Paul usually means initial forensic standing before God
In Romans 3 and 4, when Paul uses justification language, he is dealing with standing before God. The courtroom is open. The verdict is rendered. The ungodly are justified by faith apart from works of the law.

The emphasis falls on the ground of the verdict.

Christ.

Faith.

Gift.

Grace.

No boasting.

No earning.

That is Paul’s world.

James is pressing on the demonstrability of faith
James is not setting up that same courtroom in the same way.

He is pressing on whether a professed faith is vindicated, shown, displayed as real by what it does.

That is why Abraham appears in both Paul and James, but in different argumentative roles.

Paul uses Abraham to show that righteousness is counted through faith apart from works.

James uses Abraham to show that faith is active, completed, and shown in obedience.

Same figure.

Different question.

Same broad theological world.

Different pressure point.

That matters because it shows again that overlapping vocabulary does not mean identical intent.

This is why Luther misheard James
He took Paul’s problem and assumed it was James’s problem too
Now we can come back to Luther more clearly.

Luther had been rescued by Paul from a crushing religion of performance. That was real. He needed Romans. He needed justification by faith. He needed to hear that righteousness is a verdict, not a grade.

But then he came to James, heard “justified by works,” and because he assumed James must be answering Paul’s question, he heard James as undoing the gospel.

That was the mistake.

James was not answering Paul’s question.

James was answering the question of dead orthodoxy, inert confession, unembodied belief, and loveless religion.

Those are not the same thing.

So Luther, for all his brilliance, misread James because he imposed one author’s categories onto another author without asking first whether they were even solving the same problem.

That is a caution worth keeping close.

Great readers can still flatten the Bible if they are not careful
And that should humble everyone.

It means biblical faithfulness is not just about knowing theology. It is also about reading patiently enough to let one author be himself before forcing him into another author’s frame.

That is exactly what this whole series has been training.

Context before confidence.

Author before assumption.

Audience before abstraction.

Problem before prooftext.

That is how you keep from manufacturing contradictions.

Paul and James actually need each other
Paul guards the ground
Paul protects the gospel from becoming performance.

He says the sinner does not earn right standing.

He says works of the law do not justify.

He says righteousness is received by faith.

He says the verdict is given as gift.

He says boasting is excluded.

Without Paul, Christians would turn the gospel into an achievement system.

They would keep trying to earn their place.

They would live under a grade forever.

Paul says no.

The ground is Christ.

The verdict is gift.

Stand there.

James guards the evidence
James protects the gospel from becoming inert agreement.

He says faith without works is dead.

He says hearing without doing is self-deception.

He says mercy matters.

He says love of neighbor matters.

He says a profession that leaves the hungry unfed is not the kind of faith that saves.

Without James, Christians could drift into a kind of verbal orthodoxy that never moves toward love.

They could say true things about grace and never actually embody mercy.

James says no.

If faith is alive, it acts.

Not to earn the ground.

Because it is alive.

The church still needs both warnings
Some churches need Paul more
There are communities where everything turns into performance.

People are constantly measuring themselves.

Acceptance feels conditional.

Holiness becomes anxiety.

The Christian life is experienced as a treadmill of proving, trying, and fearing.

Those communities need Paul badly.

They need to hear that the verdict is in.

They need to hear that righteousness is credited.

They need to hear that justification is by faith apart from works of the law.

They need to hear that Christ is the ground.

Without that, the church becomes exhausting.

Some churches need James more
There are other communities where everyone knows the right language, but the faith itself has become soft, inert, and strangely unmoving.

The doctrine is sound.

The statements are clear.

The suffering are still ignored.

The poor are still neglected.

The life of the church is still shaped by favoritism, self-protection, and comfort.

Those communities need James badly.

They need to hear that faith without works is dead.

They need to hear that mercy is not optional.

They need to hear that saying the right things does not prove the life of God is active in you.

Without that, the church becomes correct and cold.

Many believers need both writers in the same week
And honestly, a lot of Christians need both Paul and James at once.

Because many of us are capable of both errors.

We can be performance-driven one day and complacent the next.

We can fear that we have not done enough and then also hide behind correct doctrine while doing very little.

We can need Paul to free us from self-salvation and James to expose our deadness.

That is why the New Testament gives us both.

Not because the Bible is confused.

Because the human heart is.

νόμος really is doing different things in Paul and James
In Paul, law often marks the covenantal and legal structure he is arguing against as a basis for justification
This is especially true in Romans 3 and Galatians 3.

There, νόμος is bound up with Torah, covenant obligation, curse, boasting, and exclusion. Paul can speak of the works of the law, the curse of the law, the law’s inability to justify, and the law’s role in exposing sin.

That is a specific use in a specific fight.

In James, law becomes the shape of obedient freedom and neighbor-love
James can speak of the perfect law of liberty and the royal law.

That is not because he has abandoned grace or become legalistic.

It is because he is using νόμος to describe the shape of a life ordered by God’s will, summed up in love, and expressed in action.

That is why one writer can sound more negative and the other more positive while both remain faithful.

The word is the same.

The problem is not.

The burden is not.

The pastoral aim is not.

That is what careful reading sees.

The five questions pull the whole passage together
Who wrote this?
Paul or James makes a difference.

Not because truth changes from author to author, but because emphasis does.

Paul is Paul.

James is James.

They are not interchangeable.

To whom are they writing?
Paul writes to mixed Jew-Gentile churches wrestling with covenant inclusion and boasting.

James writes to Jewish Christians who know the Scriptures and need their faith to be embodied and active.

Audience shapes vocabulary.

What problem are they solving?
Paul is fighting religion as performance and law as basis of status.

James is fighting dead faith, verbal orthodoxy, and loveless inaction.

That is the decisive difference.

What does the lexicon show?
νόμος has a range.

Torah.

Norm.

Principle.

Rule.

Requirement.

You cannot decide meaning without context.

What does the word mean elsewhere in the same book?
Paul uses νόμος in multiple ways even within Romans.

James consistently leans toward law as liberty, royal law, neighbor-love, and doing.

That internal pattern matters.

Those five questions are not just a nice method. They are the way through the passage.

Final thoughts
Paul and James are not enemies
That may be the line worth carrying away.

Paul and James are not fighting each other.

They are standing shoulder to shoulder, guarding different sides of the same gospel.

Paul says you do not earn your way in.

James says if you are really in, it will show.

Paul says the verdict is gift.

James says living faith moves.

Paul says do not build your standing on religious performance.

James says do not confuse mere profession with living faith.

Those are not opposite messages.

They are necessary companions.

The ground and the fruit must both stay clear
And maybe that is the cleanest way to hold them together.

Paul guards the ground.

James guards the fruit.

The ground is Christ, grace, gift, faith, justification, no boasting.

The fruit is love, mercy, action, obedience, care for neighbor, faith made visible.

If you turn fruit into ground, you lose the gospel.

If you deny the fruit grows from the ground, you lose living faith.

You need both.

And that is why this week matters so much.

The same word does not always mean the same thing in every mouth.

Law does not always mean law in the same way.

And once you learn to ask the right questions before making a theological conclusion, a contradiction that once felt sharp begins to resolve into something much better.

Not confusion.

Completion.
